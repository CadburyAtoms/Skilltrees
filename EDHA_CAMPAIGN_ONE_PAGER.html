1The World what everyone knows

One continent, ten nations — glacier coast in the north, mesa badlands in the south. Five leylines run through everything, everywhere. People who learn to draw on them work what everyone calls magic — trained wielders are rare, one to a village — and where a line runs strong, the land itself runs strange: beasts, even whole groves, with a color written into hide and habit. Farmers keep rules about them the way they keep rules about wolves.

Ten gods are real. They have churches, they answer prayers — some of them, some of the time — and they are far away in a way priests don't like being asked about. And times have turned hard, in ways the old folk say don't add up: blights that never burn out, sickness that lingers past all sense, raids across borders that used to be quiet. Nobody agrees on why — and, the roads being what they are, no one can truly say how far it runs. Finding out what is actually wrong with the world is the campaign.

One custom everyone shares: at any death, you pour something living-given — water, milk, a pinch of grain-ash — onto bare earth and say some version of "go back well." Ask why and you'll get a shrug: it's just what's done. But one thing has changed, and nobody discusses it: for about two years the shrine-keepers have stopped offering the oldest of their rites — the Passing, the dying's right to ask the god for a gentle end. Press a keeper on why, and you get the same tired answer the priests give about everything lately: "the rites don't take like they did."
