## The world in ten breaths

Thyrcross is one continent, ten nations, about 4,000 km top to bottom — glacier coast in the
north, mesa badlands and singing plains in the south. Five **leylines** — White, Blue, Black,
Red, Green — run through everything, everywhere; some places they run strong enough to see, and
people who learn to draw on them work what everyone calls magic. Ten **gods** are real. They
have churches, they answer prayers — some of them, some of the time — and they are far away in
a way priests don't like being asked about.

And times have turned *hard*, in ways the old folk say don't add up: blights that spread through
a field and never burn out, sickness that lingers past all sense, raids across borders that used
to be quiet. Nobody agrees on why — and, the roads being what they are, no one can truly say how
far it runs.

**One custom the whole continent shares:** at any death, you pour something living-given —
water, milk, a pinch of grain-ash — onto bare earth and say some version of *"go back well."*
Ask why and you'll get a shrug: it's just what's done.

## The ten gods (as everyone knows them)

Worship is organized and local — most nations keep one established church, or none. All ten are
real; which ones *answer* is a matter your priest gets quiet about.

| God | Domain | Called | Where the faith is strongest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Verdannis | Sovereignty | the Crowned Arbiter | Thalendor |
| Maelith | Chaos | the Unmaker of Certainties | Kettavar |
| Tessavain | Order | the Lawgiver | church networks everywhere; no home nation |
| Anaveth | Life | the Vital Hand | healers' orders, scattered |
| Morrath | Death | the Last Harvest | village shrines everywhere — every town has one |
| Gnothis | Knowledge | the Watching Mind | Malcurr; the "old ways" on the borders |
| Olvarra | Fate | the Thread-Reader | small, quiet fellowships |
| Kethane | Civilization | the Great Builder | soldiers, builders, and roadwrights everywhere |
| Tyrith | Power | the Iron Crown | ambitious courts and companies, discreetly |
| Razkael | Destruction | the Sundering Flame | nowhere for ~120 years — a curse word in Vorsk |

## The five leylines (if your character touches magic)

All five are everywhere; places and people differ in what runs strong in them. As a rough
palette: **White** — coordination, bulwark, accord. **Blue** — calculation, illusion,
foresight. **Black** — entropy, isolation, dominion. **Red** — heat, ferocity, the surge.
**Green** — growth, vitality, the wild. (Your talent choices are the mechanical version of
this; pick a color that fits who your character is, or a god whose path calls to them.)

Plenty of ordinary folk carry a *knack* — one small trick worn smooth by a trade: the
thirty-year bargemaster's river-sense, a midwife's steady hands, a sergeant's voice that
carries too well — and nobody calls it magic. Trained practitioners are rarer: one to a
village, a specialist or two to a soldier-company. Someone who walks several talents deep
into a tree — someone like you — is the kind of person stories start attaching to.

The leylines show in the land's creatures, too. Everyone knows some beasts and even some groves
run strange where a line stands strong — a lineage with the frequency written on its hide, its
habits, sometimes one uncanny trick (never true spellwork). They're uncommon but not secret:
every district has its named example, and farmers keep rules about them the way they keep rules
about wolves. Ask a herder before a scholar.

## The nations — where are you from?

One practical note before you pick: Thyrcross speaks a **common tongue**. Nations differ in
accent, idiom, and cant — bargefolk river-cant, Canticle's law-song registers — never in
whether you can understand each other.

### Kettavar — the far-north tundra

*You know you're in Kettavar when the chieftain's own hall has one wall built crooked on purpose, and nobody will tell you tomorrow's plan without casting for it first.*

The stable one — strange as that sounds for the Unmaker's own nation. Tribes under a chieftain, generations of calm, the smallest and calmest people on the map. It's a long, thin peninsula of glacier-white tundra facing the sea on three sides: herds on the inland strip, boats off the western headland, a treeless country where every roof-beam is driftwood or whalebone. To the south **Vorsk's mountain wall** rises straight off the border; the east root of the peninsula joins Malcurr, and Goldenport's hulls put in at the shore-markets.

**Omen-casting, as ordinary as weather.** No hunt departs, no marriage binds, no camp moves until a Chaos priest casts the omens — smoke, split bones, bird-flight over the glacier line. It isn't fortune-telling; it's *procedure*, unremarkable as checking the sky before you sail. Children learn casting-sticks as a game before their letters.

**The Unmaking Days.** A handful of days each year — never fixed; the omens choose them — when order is deliberately turned upside down: chieftains serve the cookfires, tools are downed, debts are read backwards. Vent the chaos in small chosen doses and it won't come for you in one large unchosen one. It has worked for generations, which is why nobody says "tomorrow I will," only "tomorrow I may."

**The open hand and the remembering.** No mint, no standing prices — wealth is herds, gifts, and remembered obligation, and a rich Kettavari is not one who *has* much but one who is *owed* much. Each midwinter every household recites what it owes and is owed — **the remembering** — no tallies, just memory witnessed by neighbors; to misremember twice in your own favor is social death. These are the debts the Unmaking Days read backwards.

**The sea and the crooked rows.** No boat puts out without a casting, and a crew that ignores a bad reading is *shunned for the catch*. What the sea gives back — wreck-timber, whale-fall — belongs to no finder until it's cast for. The lee plots under the southern wall are sown on the reading, not the calendar: barley in deliberately crooked rows, because perfect rows are bad luck and the flaw lets the change out.

**The last casting.** When the wasting catches someone and will not finish, the family asks the priest for one more reading, and the *omens* set the day. The dying one is dressed warm, given the giving-back in reverse — a sip of something living-given — and carried out at dusk to the sea-ice or high snow. No kin raises the hand and no kin chooses the day. Grief here is strangely *scheduled*, and the days before it are spent well.

**The deliberate flaw.** Every house keeps one imperfect joint, "the flaw that lets the change out"; a mended cup should show its crack. No one gives an exact herd-count — the answer is always *"about enough."* A coming-of-age youth must *break one certainty*. And the towns wear the god's name on purpose — **Maelstrand**, the chieftain's seat with its casting-ground, and **Maelvik**, the sealing-and-whaling shore — because the Unmaker's name is the one name that never *fixes* anything.

**The whiteout is full of stories.** The one every child grows up on is the **fetch** — a shape that walks out of the white-blind wearing someone you know, and seeing one is an omen of death (keep your dogs close in the white). The herds are shadowed by the **cull**, wolves that take exactly the failing animal and never a sound one — you do not drive them off. Out on the water a **toll-taker** follows the whaling crews and takes its cut of every catch; the crews treat it as a toll, not a fight. And the deep hunt is for the **greatfish**, the whale whose fall feeds a shore and whose bone becomes the roof-beams overhead.

**Names:** flowing single names, no fixed surnames — bynames change when the omens say (*Miravel*, *Oravel Longwinter*, *Kesva First-Ashore*, *Talvin*, *Nimeva*). "Who were you when…?" is a polite question. Demonym: **Kettavari**.
**You might be:** an omen-caster's apprentice sent south to read a wider sky; a youth whose coming-of-age "broken certainty" was leaving; a trader who finds the calm back home harder to explain every year; a boat-hand who owes a winter's debt the remembering hasn't collected yet.

### Malcurr — the northeast lake country

*You know you're in Malcurr when a stranger proves a claim by rolling up a sleeve, and the lakes at night are ringed with lamplight that never goes out.*

Cold upland lake country in the continent's northeast — the second-largest nation on the map, its waters strung in branching chains of glacial-valley lakes with fells and forge-towns between the arms. The lakes are the nation's **roads**, not its larder: the fisheries are thin, and boats and portages carry everything. It's ruled from lakebound **Kenmere** by the Warlock, and its southern trade road runs down into Corvaine. Plague sits heavy here, and it sits strangely — the death-vigils no longer end.

**The Proof.** Malcurri faith is experiential: the lesson you learn by burning your hand, not by reading about fire. Rank in a guild, a priesthood, or the Warlock's service is marked in **scars and brands, each testifying to a lesson lived** — a smith's forearm reads like a résumé, and oaths are sworn on one's own scars. Knowledge that cost nothing counts for nothing; *"cheap knowledge"* is the standing insult for book-learning, and the honored gift is a **lesson**, not an object.

**The Proving.** The rite behind the surnames. Not a test you sit — a lesson you must have *lived*, then attested before three provers (a guild master, a priest of the Watching Mind, and a neighbor who witnessed it): you show the scar or work and **teach the lesson back**. Acceptance is *"the proof holds,"* and the new surname is spoken aloud for the first time — a Malcurri's string of surnames is their résumé (*"Dorvek Coldweld, who was Dorvek Truehinge"*). Failure is not shame but *unfinished*. Journeymen travel abroad to live lessons home can't teach.

**The language of knowing.** Everyday speech distinguishes *book-known* from *hand-known*, and asking someone **how they learned** a thing is courtesy where asking *what* they know is faintly rude. Children collect their elders' scar-stories the way other nations' children collect war tales.

**The Lamp-tenders and the still-houses.** A vigil was once three nights so the dying were never alone at the end — but under the plague the vigils no longer finish, so vigil-keeping became a **Warlock-chartered guild**: the Lamp-tenders, who watch in shifts and wear their **years of watching as brands**. The sick go by boat to island **still-houses**, and every lake at night shows its ring of lamps; families visit from the water, a tender carrying the last few meters. The guild's creed, never written in the charter, is **no one goes out in the dark**.

**The beached fishers, and the honest light.** The catch has gone untrusted — the connected waters never seem to clear — so the fishing fleet became the ferry fleet. *"A fisher's hands know the water; the water just stopped feeding them."* And the lamps are older than the plague, because on Malcurr's water **sound lies twice over** — the hush smearing voices from above, the wrongwake throwing its splash from below — and light is the only honest signal.

**The Warlock at Kenmere.** He rules the lake-hub capital as the **Proven-of-All** — the man who has lived every lesson. His portrait-scars hang in the **Proofhall** like relics, and he has shed his birth name entirely, claiming to have proven past the need for one. His prayers to the Watching Mind are visibly *answered*, which his priests preach as proof of favor.

**The Sevenbrand.** The southern trade town of **Brandmere** holds the forge the continent knows by its mark: the **Sevenbrand**, seven strokes struck into every blade and coin. "Seven-branded steel" is the byword for the best work there is. Among Brandmere's smiths is a quiet minority devout to the Builder — guild-level, never the state church — and some forge **constructs**, metal that stands sentinel at the forge door on its own.

**What lives in the water and on the fells.** The lakes are full of liars. The **wrongwakes** — cold pike the length of a skiff — throw their sound sideways: *the splash you hear is never the fish you fear*, and hungry seasons have made the great ones bold. Overhead the **hushwings**, slate-gray dusk-swifts, fly in colonies that smear voices and bells into mush — half of why lake-folk signal with light. The blind **wake-eels** rise in a slow silent ring on the surface in the minutes around a death on the water; an eel-ring off a village always meant *send for the keeper*. The small dark **ferry-foxes** once trotted ahead of funeral processions and now pace the still-house ferries from the shore. Up on the peat moors the **fellstags** grow a hedge-maze where they tread (a hedge you don't remember is one you walk *around*), the forge-dark **brandrams** simply *charge* (stand somewhere one can't get up to speed), and the great **tussock-sows** engineer the mire itself — *a lane she wants is hers, and a lane she doesn't stops being a lane.*

**Names:** short, consonant-forward given names (*Kashen, Dorvek, Salla, Ostrek, Merin, Veska*); surnames are earned credentials, re-taken at a major Proving (*Kashen Duskhand*) — children wear a parent's name until their own first Proving, and calling a proven adult by that milk-name is a fighting insult. Demonym: **Malcurri**.
**You might be:** a Lamp-tender who has watched too long; a guild journeyman abroad to *live* a lesson no one at home can teach; a beached fisher's child who knows the water better than anyone who still trusts it; a quiet doubter of the Warlock's proofs.

### Corvaine — the eastern riverlands

*You know you're in Corvaine when the dawn bell rings and everyone turns toward the palace, where a child's small hand is held up at a balcony rail — and then everyone goes back to queueing at the plague-wells.*

The riverlands between two rivers, ruled by a child on a throne and the regents behind it. To the west runs the **Palewater**, the great border river that comes down out of **Lake Morrain** and divides Corvaine from Thalendor's forest; off that same northern lake country a second river runs south and east to the sea at **Aldercourt**, the capital — a port built on **alder piles**, the wood that does not rot underwater, in the nation where nothing seems to finish dying. The court sits some fifteen road-days from the Palewater war it funds and won't name.

**The Morning Presentation.** Every dawn Child King **Cassien II** is shown at the balcony — proof the realm still has a king, and a dawn. Acts of state are sealed with the king's small handprint in wax over the signatures of the three regents: the **Lord Chancellor**, the **Marshal-Regent**, and the **Queen Dowager**. The calendar is dense with ceremony that stands in for governance, and everything hard is deferred "until the king is grown" — a phrase Corvaini use for any hopeless postponement.

**The Tolling.** A wasting that will not finish has taught Corvaine a grim new law. Where the sickness holds a body past all use and hope, the family may petition the parish, and if the bell tolls, the sufferer is **dead-in-law**: struck from the rolls, the will executed, the widow free to remarry — while the body breathes on in a hospice house, its name spoken only in the past tense. And there are treasonous whispers, never repeated by sensible people, that the same bell was rung first and highest for the old king himself.

**Plague-wells and the painted line.** Where the plague seals a district, its wells are painted **hospice yellow** — for presumed-fouled — and a well touched by sick hands never comes clean again; everything drawn is boiled first, so the sealed streets' true shortage is not food but firewood. Where a sick district has no well, a sworn **well-warden** hauls from the nearest clean one and pours across a painted line into yellow vessels that never cross back — hand never meets hand. Every Corvaini knows the paint's arithmetic: no painted line, once drawn, has ever moved back. *"Past the paint"* is the idiom for anything beyond saving.

**Raid culture as duty.** Soldiers crossing the Palewater carry **writs of requisition** — taking with a writ is lawful duty, taking without one is banditry and hangs. Returning raiders **wash in the river before entering their village** ("leave the far bank in the water"), and are met with bowed heads no one raises. The shame is ritual precisely because everyone knows the units doing it are starving too.

**Paid in paper.** Crown coin still spends, at a sliding discount — but the treasury pays its civic posts first and the army last, in **writs of arrears**: solemn crown paper that trades at a fraction of its face. *"Paid in paper"* is Corvaini for a promise you'll never collect.

**The black stone at the river-meet.** The south-border villages nearest the confluence keep old burial rules and won't much explain them: never bury in earshot of the river, graves face *up*river, thresholds wear an iron nail wound with green thread. Ask why and you get a shrug and a changed subject.

River country keeps river creatures. Everyone knows the **tollbirds** — ash-gray carrion crows that gather on a roofline *before* a death, honest as a bell; lately, folk mutter, the flocks gather and then *don't leave*. Bargefolk watch the fog for **mistherons**, man-tall waders you hear in the wrong quarter and see a pace from where they truly stand — *you never see the one that has you* — bolder now than grandfather's were. And out on the grass the **skeindeer** run in herds a hundred strong that wheel and flee as one body, so regular the plainsfolk read them like weather; you never walk up on a skein unseen, and the herds nearest the river have lately taken to *"widow-dancing"* — drilling in rings around empty ground — which nobody watches for long.

**Names:** clipped soldier surnames and florid court names alike (*Maren Voss*, *Halden Roek*, *Ossian*).
**You might be:** a soldier who has carried one writ too many across the Palewater; a minor courtier who believes in the king the way you believe in a story; a border-village kid raised on the burial rules; someone whose parent was belled out — and still breathes.

### Thalendor — the great central forest

*You know you're in Thalendor when supper comes in courses with proud names — and every course is the same pale honey-colored loaf from the Mage's vats.*

The mage-ruled utopia in the continent's green heart, where the famous **Root Network** runs — and, devout as it is, the nation the famine has hit worst. Its eastern edge is the **Palewater**, the great border river; across it lies Corvaine, whose short-paid soldiers raid the grain roads, while to the northwest Vorsk watches the forest for the moment it staggers.

**Oaths of Station.** Sovereignty worship, lived: every working role — warden, miller, bargemaster, marshal — is a **station**, sworn before the Crowned Arbiter's altar and conferred in a small crowning rite with a circlet of the trade's own stuff (rope, iron, wheat-straw when there was wheat). To be raised or cast down from a station is liturgy, not paperwork — which is why "Marshal" or "Architect" here sits closer to a sacrament than a job.

**The formal table.** The Mage's alchemical vats are the one thing standing between the nation and open starvation, and Thalendor meets that fact with ceremony: the pale concentrate — "the Mage's bread" — is laid out as the meal it replaces, dishes named for what they imitate, and it's bad manners to say aloud what it is. The real divide is **field-fed vs. vat-fed**: a true apple is a gift of consequence, and a host who can afford it serves *one true thing*, however small — everyone at the table knows what it cost.

**The Root Network and its groves.** Where the Network runs shallow, groves are sacred — **shrine-groves**, entered barefoot, never cut, each kept by a sworn **root-warden**. The deep stands are alive in more than the usual way: **rootlings**, root-and-whip runners the size of dogs, tend the old groves, and the oldest shrine-groves are **grove-hearts** — single living stands with something like a village's mind, spoken to, never crossed. Lately, folk say, the groves have gone strange.

**Wardens on the wing and the hoof.** Two creatures every Thalendori can name. The **reeve-owl** — a canopy eagle-owl, wings shield-broad, its face ringed dark like a circlet — has always taken the forest's slow deaths, the sick hare and the failing fawn finished clean under its talons; foresters read it not as appetite but as office, the Arbiter's bailiff serving the last writ, and a beast it takes was "cast down." The **crownoxen** of the glades form **the ring** at any threat — horns out, calves and elders inside, each beast holding the same post year after year; "the crown holds" means the danger's passed. But both have gone wrong of late: the owls cull and leave their kills uneaten *like writs served*, and near the worst-starved reaches they madden and come for the dying themselves; the oxen hold rings around bare ground, and a ring is meat that won't scatter — so hungry men with pikes have begun butchering formations that will not break, and the courts argue whether that's hunting or something worse.

**The brewing heresy.** The most devout nation starves worst, and the pews have noticed. Preachers of the **Lowered Crown** offer two readings, both spreading: the Arbiter has *cast Thalendor down* for a sin unnamed — or, whispered, he is *not able*. Some districts refuse the First Sheaf rite rather than say it over a vat-loaf. Whether the Mage's alchemy is salvation or blasphemy depends on which reading you hold.

**Names:** plain given names; grove surnames or terse earned station-names (*Theron Ashmark*, *Doran Fenn*, *Vareth Khor*).
**You might be:** a root-warden of a shrine-grove gone quiet; a garrison soldier on the Palewater, the raid border; a station-sworn miller with no grain, asking the Lowered Crown question out loud; a forester who's seen the owls leave their kills and can't unsee it.

### Goldenport — the west coast

*You know you're in Goldenport when a deal isn't real until it's countersigned, witnessed, and waxed — and the winner quietly drops a coin in the poor-box "so the Luck doesn't notice."*

The guild oligarchy of the deep inlets, strung the length of the western seaboard — and, quietly, the place the hard years mostly forgot. No king and no church: a Council of guild charters rules, and the sacred thing here is paper. The Port proper is only the biggest bead on a long string — guild harbor-towns run the whole west coast and out onto the islands, and between them moves the only merchant fleet in the west. Vorsk's ranges pinch the mountains off from the sea and Lunavar's coast is a marsh-mouth sliver, so the delta rice and mountain ore of half the continent both leave under a Goldenport countersign. The Council calls this *service*; the coast calls it *the toll*.

**The charter is the altar.** The guild charter is the sacred object — read aloud in full on Charter Day with every member touching the ribbon; apprenticeships, marriages, and partnerships all made by countersigned instrument. The one unforgivable sin is oath-breaking: the guilty are **struck from the books** — their name lined out of every ledger in the city, made *unwritten*, unable to lawfully buy, sell, or witness. The docks say a signature can baptize anything; the counting-houses do not say it back.

**The Port's luck.** Gardens overgrow their plots, wounds knit clean, harbor fevers pass mild, the deep-water boats come home heavy — and everyone knows better than to *count* on it aloud. You spit-flip a coin to the harbor on sailing; you don't boast of the Luck and you don't tally it; you tithe a sliver of every windfall to the poor-box so it doesn't notice you. The Council line is smug — *good charters make good harvests* — and the counting-houses believe their own ledgers; the docks, who see the coasts they trade with, are less sure the Port did anything to deserve the difference. You see the Luck plainest in the garden district inland of the wharves: the **dooryard harts** graze unafraid and heal clean inside the charter-stones, fed by folk law as the **Garden Sow** is — a boar of impossible size and age who keeps every other predator out of the district under an old agreement no one signed: the first furrow's crop turned out at the field edge, and no hunting inside the stones.

**The First Page.** An arriving refugee is *unwritten by default* — the Port's worst punishment, handed out by accident of birth. The way up is the **First Page**: a chartered sponsor countersigns a ledger page in your name, your wage-history becomes your citizenship application, and years of clean entries buy your charter. Until then you work passage, paid in scrip against your own page. The coast's hungry and stateless come anyway, because this is the one shore that will eventually write them down.

**The Peace of the Ledger.** Even Vorsk keeps the peace here. A warband that touches chartered ground gets its whole clan's ore struck from the books — and ore that can't reach the fleet is just rock — so the raiders who harrow every other coast only ever *trade* on this one. It is the single raid that costs too much.

**The deep-water fleet.** The fleet's deepest advantage isn't cargo: the Port's blue-water boats fish grounds no inshore skiff can reach, and of late come home heavy in years when other coasts' catch is whispered about — clean fish the hungry border buys back at toll prices (the docks' uneasy line: *"we sell the fen their own supper"*). What the boats fear out there is the **keelshadow**, a shape that paces a hull from below and takes fish off the long-lines; you *pay it off the ledger*, the first fish of every set over the side uncounted, and keelshadow sign is read as good fishing. No skipper will harm a **silverwake** — the white harbor porpoises that ride the bow-waves are the Luck's own watchers. North, where Vorsk's hills run down to the water, the beaches smoke at dawn: **cinderbrocks**, furnace-hot digging beasts that pile dry wrack over the mussel beds and strike sparks to pop the shells; even the Vorsk carters won't kill one — *you don't kick the hearth*.

**The coin is a ledger you can carry.** Goldenport mints the continent's cross-border money — "port coin" — trusted not for its metal but because every striking run is a *chartered instrument*, and debasing one is oath-breaking. Its own names for the issue are the **stroke** (copper), the **seal** (silver), and the **charter** (gold): *"thirty seals, port"* is how a factor says thirty silver. Each run's milled **ribbon-edge** is unique, and *"check the ribbon"* is continental for *make sure it's real*.

**Names:** short given + ledger surnames; mononyms for those "the books know" (*Lysa Venn*, *Serene*).
**You might be:** a guild courier who has seen what the rest of the coast looks like; an unwritten debtor working passage; a dockside healer whose hands work better than they should.

### Vorsk — the northwest mountains

*You know you're in Vorsk when raiders empty your granary with courteous precision — and leave the seed grain untouched, because taking it would shame their warband.*

The warlord state everyone else calls bandit country, wrongly. Vorsk is the **continental roof** — high valleys, thin soil, long winters, a wall of mountain country in the far northwest whose rivers run down out of the passes to feed the lowlands. The ranges pinch the valleys off from the sea, so everything Vorsk digs — ore, mostly — leaves by one road down to one coast, a fact that shapes half its politics. To the north the mountain wall rises off Kettavar's border; to the south lies the marsh country the warbands raid.

**The Taking-law.** What a raid may take: stores, stock, coin — *in shares, never all*. What it may never take: **seed grain, the tools of a trade, or the last of anything** — "eating the foot you stand on," the old folk call it. Captives owe work-debt or ransom, never slavery. And burning what you cannot carry is the deepest dishonor: destruction without taking is *waste*, and waste, people still say — spitting — is **the Flame's work**. A warband that steps over your unlocked seed-chest and takes only its share has told you what it is: warband, not bandit.

**The warband and the share.** You are born to a household but at adolescence you *join a warband* — guild, regiment, and second family at once. A newcomer spends a season as a **carried name**, holding no share, until the autumn reckoning grants the **first share** that makes you real; from that day you are *X of [warband]*. The share *is* the economy — Vorsk mints no coin, and everything owed (ransom, bride-price, blood-price) is quoted in shares of the band's book; *"he sold his share"* is the worst thing one Vorsker can say of another. Life happens in **steadings** — forts in miniature, forty to eighty hearths behind one wall and one gate, the seed grain in an unlocked chest beside it. Twice a year the chiefs ride to **Kragmoot** for the **moots of thaw and frost**; the Warlord is just the chief whose warband holds the high hall, his seat won by **challenge**.

**The going-up.** When the wasting takes a Vorsker — the cough that won't finish, the age that won't close — the household does not nurse it for years the way the river nations do. When the dying one is ready they make **the asking**, spoken before two witnesses *not of their own hearth*, ending with the old formula — *"carry me up."* Their **warband** carries them, never their kin, up past the winter line to the range's **quiet shelves**, dressed in their best, armed, and sung to — then the living walk down and leave the mountain to finish it. No herder grazes a quiet shelf; no carrion bird circles one, and nobody asks why. **Shelf-iron** — a blade left with the dead — even a starving warband won't touch. The custom's quiet horror is the weather: as the hard years stack, the winter line creeps lower, and the walk up gets shorter.

**The Peace of the Ledger.** One coast no warband will raid: Vorsk's ore is worthless without Goldenport's fleet, and the Guild strikes a raiding clan's whole ore-trade out of its books, so a raid on the Port starves the raider's own valley by spring. Coast duty — guarding clerks instead of winning shares — is **"wearing the pen,"** a soft posting rotated by lot because none will volunteer. The raiding energy goes around instead, south into the fen. **Tirgard** is the gate-town where the ore road starts down; **Kragmoot**, the high hall on its crag, is the capital.

**The dead god you don't kick.** At the passes stand broken shrines of the **Sundering Flame** that no one prays at and every warband still tidies in passing — "you don't feed a dead fire, but you don't kick the hearth either." The faith has been silent for as long as the oldest steadings can name, and its god's name survives mostly as profanity: *"Flame take it."* Nobody quite remembers why it went quiet, and nobody prays to find out. And then, of late, something new: a church of the **Iron Crown** has taken root in the high halls and spreads warband to warband — command preached as holy, challenge-succession made scripture, growing faster than any faith has a right to. Its chaplains ride the raids now, offering what the share-book never can: rank with no ceiling. The Warlord tolerates it because it flatters the institution that crowned him — and the old folk mutter that *the sun was warlord enough*.

**The high country and its beasts.** Up in the crags run the **crag drakes** — dragons, if you ask a shepherd — pack lizard-kin that read a herd and take its sickest animal, half-blessed for the culling; bolder of late, and lower than they used to come. A steading's proudest animal is its **bellwether**, the pale-eyed lead-ewe a whole flock follows like one body; when a flock stops following her, *"the bell goes unanswered"* and the herder knows the sickness is in it. And the valleys keep their wolves, the **cull**, that take only the failing beast and never a sound one.

**Names:** short, hard given + one-syllable kin name; formally *"X of [warband]"*, warbands named as **folds** and **hundreds** (*Isra Vael*; *Berrek Karn* of the Stonefold; *Ferrik Cade*).
**You might be:** a warband second who broke the Taking-law, or refused to; a shrine-tender's grandchild who wants to know why the Flame went quiet; a raider grown tired of the southern marshes; a chaplain of the new church, certain the god's rise is your own; the kin of someone who was carried up.

### Lunavar — the western marshes

*You know you're in Lunavar when the villages sleep through noon and the markets open at moonrise — and every settlement keeps one pool of water that nobody may touch.*

The theocracy that lives by night, and the continent's rice country. Its western marshes drain seaward by two slow outlets; the capital, **Moonmere**, sits far to the south on its own inlet, with the whole depth of the fen between it and the northern border — because Lunavar is raided from the north by Vorsk, and the raid-front town of **Fenholt** takes the brunt. The famine bites hard: paddies gone to blight, the marsh larder distrusted, and the priesthood has folded the hunger into the calendar.

**The Lantern's hours.** Work happens by the moon's light, "the Lantern's hours," and the month runs on its phases — assembly, justice, and the reading of the month's prophecy at the full moon; silence and fasting at the dark. Everyone knows the older reason under the holy one: by day the great **noonwings** ride the thermals over the open fen and stoop on anything that moves in rhythm — a transplanting line, a marching column, a poled boat's wake. Lunavar worked at night long before anyone called it holy. The counterplay is bred into every child: travel *broken* — scattered, out of step — or under fog, and watch the **stitchbird** flocks, whose evening murmurations the folk read like weather and whose sudden scattering is the fen's free alarm: *a broken stitch means wings up, get under cover*.

**Still water is scripture.** The moon is the Lantern — the one witness that never blinks — and prophecy is the art of reading what it has seen in its reflection. So scripture is *still water*: every settlement consecrates a **moon-pool** and takes its readings from the moon's face on the surface, and a broken reflection is a dire omen. The pools are open to the sky by rule (walls to the knee, never a lid), and the clergy who read them are **pool-readers**. Petitions are floated out onto the pools in shallow bowls.

**The Child and the Once-Children.** The Child of Prophecy is always an actual child — grown people learn to look away, and a child who hasn't yet is the only honest reader. The office ages out when the readers judge a Child has begun *expecting* what it will see; the next is found by **convergence**, distant pools returning the same sign on the same night to name a district, then a village, then a child. Aged-out Children join the college that raised them, **the Once-Children**, who govern in the current Child's name from Moonmere. Pool-readers still argue over the season the readings turned to grief — a famous shared reading nobody has ever settled — and since then dire omens have run epidemic and the readings grown harder to trust.

**The pool nobody may touch.** Every moon-pool is consecrated the same way: spring-fed, walled to the knee, never fished, never waded, never drunk from by man or beast. Six generations of that piety turned out to be quarantine — when the marsh fouled, the moon-pools were the one water nothing living had ever entered, and they are now the last clean water in much of Lunavar. So a rite exists that every reader hates — **the Lantern's mercy**, rare formal leave to draw a consecrated pool for the sick, because every pool drawn is one more eye closed in a country already drowning in bad signs. And there is a dread the college will not discuss aloud: a reader who *enters* the water — falls, is pushed, wades in on a dare or a grief — has faced the Lantern from the wrong side of the glass. The old readers say such a one surfaces carrying a reading never meant for a single pair of eyes, too much and too tangled to speak, and is never quite trusted at a pool again. Most are quietly retired; a few walk out into the fen and are not followed. Whether that is punishment, madness, or revelation, the college does not say.

**Paddy, cache, and token.** Paddy rice climbs every rise and levee, transplanted by torchlight and moon; the markets open at moonrise, and there the money is **cache-tokens**, temple-struck tallies redeemable from hidden **stilt-caches** of grain, worthless to any raider who can't find the cache. The famine has been liturgized: the college sets the month's fasts as much from the granary ledgers as from the readings, and the fast-calendar is, functionally, the ration book. Most days are fast-days now. The delta's one deep-water mouth is not Lunavar's, so rice bound for the sea is poled to the western sliver and sold into Goldenport bottoms — and the coin comes straight back, because with the marsh catch untrusted the same towns buy the Port's clean fish: rice out, fish in, both through the same countersign.

**The marsh is the wall.** Against Vorsk, the fen itself is the fortress: rice cached on hidden stilt-platforms, causeways kept as village secrets, raiders who chase too deep simply not coming back. A village in good standing has living geography on its side, its levees quietly mended by the **fen-hearts and their reedlings**, the reed-and-willow tangles that weir the water for anyone who keeps the old bargains. And the marsh's oldest safety rule doubles as catechism: **trust no light that moves.** The Lantern hangs still; a light that walks is lying — the **drownlights**, cold marsh-things that drift a false gleam just off the safe path. Newer dangers walk the same water: the **stillback**, a barge-long crocodilian whose stillness reads as a mudbank or old causeway stone until you step onto it.

**The Still Acre.** Each village keeps one dry rise for its dead — often the best-draining ground it owns, never planted, its crown kept bare earth, the keeper's low stone house at its edge. Six generations of thin margins never farmed the Still Acres; the famine has the paddies creeping up their flanks now, and everyone can see how that argument ends. On a rise above flat marsh a burial mound carries for miles — in Lunavar, the dead are becoming a skyline.

**Names:** moon-names, given at the first full moon after birth (*Selka*, *Meriv*, *Naul*, *Ysel*, *Sorne*).
**You might be:** a pool-reader who went into the water — fell, or was pushed — and surfaced carrying something you cannot yet put into words, and cannot stop trying to; a pool-reader's acolyte who saw something in the reflection nobody will discuss; a causeway-keeper who's fought Vorsk with nothing but geography; a doubter for whom the fast-days stopped feeling holy; a Fenholt raid-orphan who came south to be taken into the temple granary.

### Canticle — the southeast plains

*You know you're in Canticle when a farmer hums his lease to check the terms, and a lawsuit is two advocates singing precedents at each other until one melody carries.*

The Bards Congress aristocracy beyond the great river — the biggest land on the map, prosperous on its rim, neutral, no established church, and the closest thing the continent has to a court of appeal. It's a **rim nation wrapped around a dead heart**: the farmed country is all coastal and riverine edge, and the whole interior is **the Hush**, the great salt pan where sound dies and no witness stands. The capital **Arcanta** sits a day's ride inland of the sea port of **Portavere** — the Deep Stacks want the dry air. West, on the Ashkar border, lies closed **Lake Vespera**.

**The Sounding.** Law binds only when it is **performed** — sung or recited by a seated Bard before witnesses. Precedent is repertoire, a Bard's rank is the body of law they can perform from memory, and disputes are settled by **citation duel**: competing precedents sung before the Congress, which rules on which one *carries*. Ordinary folk carry the habit — contracts are hummed to fix a debt in memory, and a funeral is the dead one's *record*, their deeds recited in order, nothing glossed.

**The archive that keeps everything.** Arcanta holds the **Deep Stacks**: archives that preserve *everything*, understanding optional, preservation absolute — copied out by young bards who earn their seats with transcription years and are taught **never to editorialize**. The shelves are laid with salt-paper against the damp, so those years include a **salt season** at the Hush's rim springs. "The law lives where nothing rots" is how Arcanta explains sitting its whole memory on the edge of a salt pan.

**The Hush, and the law's edge.** The pan swallows sound and holds no witnesses — and since law exists only when performed *before* witnesses, the interior is **unwitnessed ground**; *"gone to the Hush"* means beyond any court's reach. So every caravan hires a **way-witness**, a licensed junior bard whose function is to be the law's presence on the road: each night-camp bargain is sung to them and carried in repertoire until deposited at a bench. Killing a way-witness is the worst crime on the plains — it unmakes every agreement the caravan carries.

**The rainroads.** The dry-season caravan roads are the **rainroads**: the gravel beds of the seasonal washes that die into the Hush, walkable only when empty. A caravan on them is a walking chorus — the beasts are led by song — and the interior's true killer isn't thirst but the flash flood that fills a rainroad without warning.

**The call-lines.** Out on the range, herds are owned by **melody**: stock are raised answering their family's **call-line** at the watering, so a rustled beast takes a season to retrain. Disputes end the Canticle way — both claimants **sing the herd in**, and whoever's line the animals answer, owns them.

**The First Pinch, and the arbiter's seat.** Every salt load sold opens with a pinch cast on the buyer's threshold — *"to keep the words of the house."* Peace, neutrality, and salt on every table keep Canticle rich, but its pride is the bench: the great river empties through a mouth Canticle does not own, and a sung compact — the **Treaty of the Mouth**, renewed as a duet each season's turning — keeps its passage free and seats it as the river's arbiter. Foreign disputes come here to be judged. Quote accurately in Canticle and doors open; misquote and they close.

**The plains and their beasts.** The range keeps uncanny company. **Callthieves** are lean predators whose one trick is to learn a herd's call-line and sing it back a hair flat, cutting out the beasts that answer — so families rotate their lines by season and sing *with* their stock at dusk; plainsfolk swear the old ones have lately begun stealing scraps of way-witness verse. Out on the flats rides **the shimmer** — a held mirage kept steady at the exact distance a failing traveler will walk, which is why any water you did not chart, you sing out to the way-witness before you walk to it: if no second voice confirms the spring, it isn't one. At the pan's edge run the **dirgehounds**, whose night-song folk hear as the Hush reading its ledger aloud; the caravans ride **saltstriders**, tall flightless birds broad-footed for the crust, and read the dawn murmurations of the **salt-larks** off the brine springs for the coming weather.

**Names:** melodic given names, cited formally with one's bench or office (*Solenne*, *Veradis*). Demonym: **Canticli**.
**You might be:** a transcription-year bard who read something odd in the Stacks and can't unremember it; a way-witness whose caravan died and left them carrying bargains no bench will ever hear; an advocate who lost a citation duel to a better melody and a worse cause; a range-herder whose family's call-line answers to someone else now.

### Sylvaneth — the eastern island

*You know you're in Sylvaneth when the road you walked in on isn't the road behind you, and your trade partner repays your small kindness to the exact measure, coin for coin.*

The fae nation off the mainland's east coast, at peace, and mostly a closed door. Trade happens at the **Strand**, the one permitted shore-port; inland isn't forbidden so much as it simply doesn't *happen* — paths end differently than they began, and the few visitors who pressed on report a polite escort back to the Strand without ever seeing who decided. What outsiders learn of the fae is only what traders and envoys see from the shore.

**Exact reciprocity.** The fae keep measure: every gift returned in kind and quantity, every favor balanced, nothing owed and nothing carried. Mortals first read it as cold; traders learn to read it as *safe* — a people who never leave a thread hanging. Their wares carry the same signature: famous dyes, instruments, seedstock — and yet nothing Sylvaneth sells takes root elsewhere, the seeds one-season sterile, the wonders never quite propagating beyond the island.

**Guarded names, and the withdrawn ones.** Names are exchanged carefully and never freely — a use-name may be offered, a true name is not, and traders learn not to press. Exile is vanishingly rare and absolute: the exiled are escorted to the Strand, given passage and provision, and then their names are *withdrawn*. Traders notice there are names Sylvaneth partners will not say.

**Names:** guarded single use-names + earned epithets (*Ashara the Unbroken* — a name traders notice Sylvaneth partners will not say).
**You might be:** a Strand-side trader fluent in fae courtesy; a mortal raised near the island with a debt repaid to the coin and a question that wasn't; (with GM buy-in) someone bound to an exile's story.

### Ashkar — the southwest badlands and desert coast

*You know you're in Ashkar when your host pours you water before asking your name — and serves it in a cup mended twice, under a roof built to be struck and moved by morning.*

The nation that isn't one anymore. It was once the Flame's own country — the one land that worshipped the Sundering Flame, a faith of honest clearing and the controlled burn — but the church died a lifetime and a half ago, and the state came apart after it: the great waterworks failed faster than anyone could rebuild them, the thirst-years came, and the holds went their own way. **Ask five people why and get five answers.** What's left is a great dry land carrying the smallest people on it — warlord holds gathered around what standing water there is, camp-towns on the roads, a desert coast where people fish and get by. The holds cluster on the **closed lakes** — mountain-locked desert waters that never reach the sea: **Kaelmere**, and **Lake Vespera** out on the Canticle border. The culture is the culture of *after*.

**Stone is for the dead.** Ashkar's deepest habit: build light, build to mend, never trust a made thing to outlast its maker. Houses are tent-and-frame even in the towns; goods are made to be repaired; wealth is how little you've tied down. The ancestors built great — the dams, the channels, the royal seat — and watched it all crumble inside a lifetime, so the surviving wisdom is the opposite of a monument. Nobody moves into the old grandeur; the ruins get quarried for gravel and never reoccupied.

**The Water-Peace.** A stateless people's portable law, and on a desert coast its currency is water. Pour a stranger a drink and have them take it, and you are both bound — no violence, no theft, safe passage — until you part at the next water. It is older than any hold and honored by all, because a coast where the Water-Peace fails is a coast no one can cross. Break it and every hold turns on you at once. Road-towns keep a **peace-stone** at the well, where the drink is shared and disputes are heard.

**The Taking-in.** Hands are scarcer than land, so a hold does not raid people — it *courts* them. A family arriving at a hold's water is a windfall, and holds openly bid: a better water-share, a mended roof, a place at the fire. To be taken in is to owe the hold your hands for a season and be owed its protection in turn, re-cut each year at low-water. Nobody is kept who can walk away — so holds compete on *treatment*. Ashkar's cruelty was never chains; it's the hold that lets its water fail and watches its people go.

**The Flame kept as ash.** The god is long gone, but the faith survives worn into gesture. Where the keepers of the quiet faith are absent — and they mostly are — the eldest still does **the clearing** for the dying who ask it: quick, witnessed at dusk, the body given to bare earth with the giving-back poured over. It is the one rite every hold keeps exactly the same. The old husbandry survives too — you fire a spent field or a plague-house down to bare ground and start clean, *"what won't mend, clear"* — and so does the bluntness: an Ashkari won't prop a failing deal with comfortable lies. Even the continental curse *"Flame take it"* lands differently here; it was their god once, so it falls somewhere between a curse and a prayer.

**The mesas and their beasts.** Away from the water it is hard country — dry washes that flood without warning, salt flats that blind and preserve, and a deep interior everyone gives a wide berth and calls bad-luck country. The beasts run strange out there, worse the deeper you go. Caravan folk fear **the reckoning**, desert coursers that hunt not by speed but by flawless coordination — they cut the line and close as one mind, so the road is theirs after dark. The deep mesas hide the **hazewyrms**, dust-dragons that wrap themselves in their own boiling heat-shimmer until no one can fix where they stand, then strike. On the open flats the **slagbulls** run hot and territorial in the killing noon; and at the lake shallows the **falsewater** shows depths that aren't there and shallows that are. Of late the wild herds the country lives off have gone murrain-sick and won't finish dying, and a hold whose stock sickens is a hold whose people walk to the next water.

**Names:** those who stayed keep a short, hard given name and no surname — you're *"[name] of [hold]"* or *"off [road]"*, and it changes as you're taken in. The towns wear the old god's name worn smooth — **Kaelmouth**, the bay port and the diaspora's door out; **Raskeld**, the dead royal seat on the western lake; **Kaelgate**, the trade door to Thalendor. The diaspora abroad wears any nation's names, loose.
**You might be:** a child of a hold that stayed — a hard past and no love of warlords; a child of the exodus, twice displaced, carrying fragments of every nation's customs; a water-keeper of a hold whose spring is failing; an eldest who has done the clearing more than once and dreams of it; someone who owed a hold a season's hands and walked off the road instead.

## The quiet faith (everyone grew up with this)

Whatever your nation and whatever your church, the Last Harvest was part of your childhood:
the low stone shrine at the burial ground's edge with its black-and-green threshold; the
keeper — no rank, no order, made by another keeper — who sits with the dying, keeps the roll
of the dead, and is fed by custom at every funeral meal. You know the rites the way you know
weather. **The Passing:** the dying, and only the dying, could ask the Shepherd to come *now,
and gently* — and she did; no hand may do by knife what the god does by grace. **The
Giving-Back:** three pours over the grave — water, milk, grain-ash — *go back well*. **The
Standing Sheaf:** the last of the year's work left untaken, the year's dead read one name to
a breath, last year's sheaf burned to make the coming year's ash. And every dusk, **the name
at the lamp**: one of your dead, said into the light as it's lit. Travelers carry a pinch of
earth from a grave they can't visit and give it to a keeper on the road, so no one is buried
only in one place. Of late, keepers have stopped offering the Passing, and if you
ask why you'll get the same quiet your priest gives — *the rites don't take like they did*.
The name at the lamp goes on; it never asked for an answer.

## Picking a faith (optional, quick guide)

Playing devout is welcome and mechanical (the deity talent paths). A quick coherence guide:
Verdannis reads truest in Thalendor; Maelith in Kettavar; Gnothis in Malcurr or as the
borderers' "old ways"; Kethane among soldiers and builders anywhere; Tessavain through the
law-church network anywhere; Anaveth through healers' orders; Morrath at every village shrine
(the quiet, universal funerary faith); Olvarra in small fellowships; Tyrith discreetly, near
power. Razkael has had no living church in six generations — a Razkael-touched PC is a
conversation with the GM, not a congregation.

## Money in Thyrcross (added with canon §5d — rulings 54–59)

**The coins are copper, silver, and gold** — 10 copper to a silver, 10 silver to a gold — and
nearly all of it is **"port coin,"** minted by Goldenport's Guild Council, whose ledger-law
makes it the one money every border trusts. Goldenport folk have their own names for the coins —
the *stroke*, the *seal*, and the *charter*, after the pen-work that backs them — and traders
everywhere say "check the ribbon": each striking run's milled edge is unique, and a clipped
ribbon means a bad coin.

**What things cost** (in a normal market — famine country is another story): a day's plain labor
1 silver · a loaf 1 copper · a meal and a bed 5 copper · river passage 1 silver a day · a plain
sword 2 gold · a trained horse 8–10 gold.

**Money is not the same everywhere.** Everyone knows: Malcurr's coin carries its forge's marks
like a smith carries scars — good coin, and it never forgets where it was struck (a Malcurri
character can name the forge-town from the marks on a coin or a blade). Corvaine's crown coin
spends at a discount, and its soldiers are paid in *writs of arrears* — solemn paper worth a
fraction of its face; "paid in paper" means a promise you'll never collect. In Thalendor the
Mage's station-sealed **ration chits** are the working money and real food outprices coin — same
in Lunavar, where the temples issue grain-backed **cache-tokens** at the moonrise markets.
Vorsk counts wealth in **warband shares**, not coin; Kettavar barely uses money at all; the fae
of Sylvaneth take port coin at the Strand and repay every kindness to the exact measure.

## Starting wealth & kit (character creation)

**Every new character starts with:** travel clothes, a bedroll-and-pack, **5 silver** (its form
follows your homeland — clean port coin; forge-marked Malcurri coin; a Corvaini's 3 silver plus
a 2-silver-face writ worth what anyone will pay; Thalendori chits plus 2 silver hard coin;
Vorsker or Kettavari goods and obligations of equal worth), **one weapon** you actually have the
skill or expertise to use (up to 2 gold — swaps at equal-or-under are fine), and your **heroic
path's kit**:

- **Agent** — picks, seal-impression wax, lamp-black, a hooded cloak, and **papers, genuine or
  otherwise**. Your key talent rewards taking chances; this kit is a license to attempt them.
- **Envoy** — one good set of fine clothes (worth more than your sword), writing kit, seal wax,
  and a **letter of credit for 1 gold** drawn on a Goldenport factor-house. Paper that turns
  into money anywhere the standard reaches.
- **Hunter** — bow and full quiver, skinning knife, leathers, rope, snares, flint, **a week of
  trail rations**, and a **spyglass and quarry-log** — the finding-and-studying tools of Seek
  Quarry made physical.
- **Leader** — sidesword, leathers, a **pennant or signal horn**, and a **written commission**:
  the company you once led — disbanded, dead, or left behind (write one line about which).
- **Scholar** — staff or knife, journal, chalk, measuring cord, candles, and a **traveling
  library of three slim volumes** (name each subject). Erudition's ranks reassign after a long
  rest: which book did you read at camp?
- **Warrior** — your weapon-slot weapon, leathers **plus a shield** (or the two-handed grip),
  whetstone, mess kit, and a regimental or warband token of wherever you learned it.
